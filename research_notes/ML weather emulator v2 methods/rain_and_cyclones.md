# ML methods for heavy precipitation and tropical cyclone track/intensity (2022-2026), applied to our 1-degree daily India emulator

Scope note: about 18 search/fetch calls. Several primary papers were paywalled (Wiley/AGU and Nature returned 403 or a login redirect), so some numbers come from abstracts, publisher blogs or search snippets. Each such case is flagged. "Our system" means: 32x36 grid at 1 degree, daily means of 6 surface variables, about 4,700 ERA5 training days, 10 autoregressive 1-day steps, CPU under 5 s, deterministic, and locality tests (day-1 response stays near the shocked box; spread of at most 8 cells/day).

## Q1. Precipitation-specific ML: which losses, heads and architectures improved heavy-rain metrics, and by how much?

### Takeaway
Global ML weather models (GraphCast, Pangu, FuXi, AIFS) get mean rainfall about right but systematically underpredict heavy and extreme rain, including over the Indian monsoon. The most reliable fixes for a deterministic model are: (a) a loss that targets threshold skill directly, such as a differentiable (soft) CSI/F1 term (up to +43.6% heavy-rain CSI in one nowcasting study); (b) likelihood-based zero-inflated heads (Bernoulli-gamma, hurdle, Tweedie), which beat MSE for extremes; and (c) upper-quantile (pinball) heads used as risk predictors. Generative models (DGMR, NowcastNet, diffusion) help most with sharpness and realism, but they are stochastic and costly, so they fit our constraints poorly.

### Cited Findings
**Evidence that AI global models underpredict extreme rain over India**
- MAUSAM (Gupta, Sheshadri, Suri; arXiv Sep 2025) evaluated FourCastNet, FourCastNet-SFNO, Pangu, GraphCast, Aurora, AIFS and GenCast over the South Asian monsoon against station, rain-gauge and satellite observations. The models underpredict extreme precipitation (a systematic finer-scale error) and show "divergent cyclone tracks". Errors against observations are 15-45% larger than errors against reanalysis. AIFS was the most consistent model; GraphCast and GenCast were also strong. — [arXiv 2509.01879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01879)
- Per the search summary of the same paper (full text not read by me): GraphCast "notably underestimates extreme precipitation (tail)" in all four Indian states studied, even though it gave the most accurate precipitation across lead times. The models collectively missed a late-July extreme event recorded by IMD. — [arXiv 2509.01879 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01879)
- GraphCast against ERA5 and IMERG for the Indian summer monsoon (2021-2024): wet bias over the core monsoon region, strong suppression of rainfall variance, extremes underrepresented. **Flag:** the abstract and full-text versions report different variance ratios (about 0.14 vs about 0.63), so do not quote either number without checking. — [arXiv 2607.11905](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11905); [html](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11905)
- EGU26 abstract on the 2023 monsoon: AI models have lower RMSE and higher pattern correlation than IFS, but they smooth precipitation at longer leads (positive S in SAL verification), and FuXi has a systematic dry bias. This is a conference abstract and unverified in detail. — [EGU26-7223](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-7223.html)
- Biparjoy (Arabian Sea, June 2023) extreme rain: per a search snippet, GraphCast reproduced the extreme rainfall best among the AI models tested. Primary source not verified. — via [WOSC 2026 abstract book](https://wosc.in/BOA_Finalised.pdf) (snippet attribution uncertain)
- Record-breaking extremes: ECMWF HRES "still consistently outperforms" state-of-the-art AI models. — [arXiv 2508.15724](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.15724)

**Loss functions targeting heavy rain**
- Differentiable CSI/F1 loss (Ko et al., "Effective training strategies for deep-learning-based precipitation nowcasting and estimation", Computers & Geosciences 2022). CSI and F1 counts are replaced by differentiable approximations, and the loss is the negative mean of these over thresholds. Reported result: up to 43.6% higher CSI for heavy rain (>= 10 mm/h) at 5-h lead, compared with focal and cross-entropy losses. The authors attribute the gain to easing class imbalance. — [arXiv 2202.10555](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10555); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S009830042200036X)
- Weather and Forecasting (2026), 24-h extreme-precipitation post-processing with a conditional GAN plus a differentiable CSI term (building on Larraondo et al.'s categorical-skill optimisation). It improved frequency and accuracy over the baseline, but caused underdispersion in probabilistic output. — [WAF 41(2), WAF-D-24-0199.1](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/41/2/WAF-D-24-0199.1.xml)
- "Advanced torrential" (AT) loss (arXiv 2025) addresses CSI's limitations with a binary penalty. About 42% higher CSI at the last lead time, but only for a single heavy-rain case, so the evidence is weak. — [arXiv 2509.01348](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01348)
- Multi-quantile pinball loss vs MSE and MAE (arXiv 2026, nowcasting): higher quantiles act as risk-sensitive predictors and "substantially" improve detection metrics at high thresholds. Exact numbers not extracted. — [arXiv 2605.30122](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.30122)
- Bernoulli-gamma negative log-likelihood (Baño-Medina et al. 2022 lineage) beats MSE across all precipitation levels for CNN downscaling, though extremes are still not fully captured. A Spain intercomparison found that MSE under-represents extremes and variability; it compared MSE, square-root, asymmetric and Bernoulli-gamma losses. — [arXiv 2411.05850](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.05850); [AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/30193/32118)
- Tweedie loss for zero-inflated rain in diffusion downscaling (Beijing): extreme recall about 0.60 vs 0.50 at the 99th percentile, plus better wet-pixel MAE. Preprint. — [arXiv 2509.08369](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08369)
- Hurdle model (logistic occurrence plus log-normal amount) estimated by a DNN for precipitation super-resolution (Vandal et al.). This is the same family as our two-part head. — [par.nsf.gov](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10358709)
- I found no head-to-head benchmark that ranks focal vs quantile vs soft-CSI vs Bernoulli-gamma losses on the same extreme-rain dataset. Reported gains come from different datasets and lead times and are not comparable. — (gap; see search above)

**Generative and physics-conditioned precipitation models (nowcasting)**
- NowcastNet (Zhang et al., Nature 619:526-532, 2023) is a physics-conditional generative model. A neural "evolution" network does advection, and a generative network adds convective detail, trained end to end over 2048 x 2048 km domains for up to 3 h. In blinded evaluations by 62 meteorologists it ranked first in about 70% of extreme-precipitation cases. Motivation: purely data-driven models "fail to obey" advective conservation. — [Nature PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06184-4.pdf); [Berkeley news](https://engineering.berkeley.edu/news/2023/07/nowcasting-model-enables-forecasting-of-extreme-precipitation/)
- On 30 heavy-rain events, NowcastNet beat HRRR at 1 h on all metrics, but HRRR and plain advection had lower RMSE at 2-3 h (per a search snippet of a follow-up paper). — [arXiv 2407.11317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11317)
- DGMR (Ravuri et al. 2021, outside our 2022+ window) ranked first in 89% of cases with 50+ Met Office forecasters. Later work notes it produces "unnatural motion and intensity" and large dissipation for extremes at longer leads. — per a search snippet citing [NowcastNet PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10356617)
- MetNet-3 (arXiv 2306.06079): beats HRRR for the first 18 h at 4 mm/h, and beats ENS over all 24 h at 8 mm/h (CSI). Exact CSI values not extracted. — [arXiv 2306.06079](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.06079)

### Inferences
- Our heavy-rain CSI of about 0.10 at 10 mm/day is consistent with the documented smoothing and tail underprediction of deterministic MSE-type models. The 3x weight on cells over 10 mm/day is a crude version of the weighted-MSE family. A **soft-CSI term at the 10 mm/day threshold** directly optimises the grading metric. Example: sigmoid((pred - 10)/tau) as soft hit probability, with TP/FP/FN as sums and loss = 1 - TP/(TP+FP+FN), added to the existing loss with a small weight. It is deterministic, costs nothing at inference, and is the best-evidenced lever (Ko et al. 2022). Watch for over-forecasting (frequency bias above 1). Monitor the bias score alongside CSI.
- The hard gate with straight-through gradients may be suppressing marginal heavy-rain cells. Two options: a soft gate (rain = p(rain) * amount) in training with a hard gate only at inference, or training the amount head with a Bernoulli-gamma or Tweedie NLL instead of weighted MSE in sqrt-space. Both are cheap, and the likelihood losses have evidence of better extremes than MSE.
- A high-quantile (e.g. q = 0.8-0.9) amount head, used only when the occurrence probability is high, is a deterministic way to counter the "everything regresses to the mean" tail loss. It may raise CSI at the cost of RMSE, so check against the grading weights.
- Generative or diffusion rain models (DGMR, NowcastNet's generative half, CorrDiff-type) conflict with "deterministic" and "CPU < 5 s". Only the advection half of NowcastNet is portable.

### Gaps
- No primary-source numbers for precipitation diagnostic heads in GraphCast, AIFS or FuXi (how tp is trained and its CSI). Not fetched within the budget.
- No primary evidence on lead-time-dependent thresholds for ML rain models.
- No paper found that tests these losses at daily, 1-degree resolution over India.

## Q2. Tropical cyclone track and intensity in ML weather models: evaluations, fixes, and North Indian Ocean evidence

### Takeaway
At 0.25 degrees, ML models (GraphCast, Pangu, FourCastNet, AIFS, Aurora) match or beat operational NWP on TC track, but they weaken storms (large low intensity bias, worst in the first 24 h). The 2025-2026 fixes that worked are: (1) TC-specific co-training on IBTrACS (WeatherNext Cyclones: 5-day ensemble-mean track error 230 km vs 370 km for ECMWF ENS); (2) a cheap post-hoc intensity-correction model (ECMWF AIFS-TC: intensity error about 3x smaller, wind bias from -29 kt to -2 kt); and (3) an explicit learned advection operator at 1 degree (PARADIS), which matches 0.25-degree models on track. North Indian Ocean evidence is thin: one preliminary abstract (Biparjoy, Nisarga, Remal, Dana) and no Amphan study of Pangu, GraphCast or FourCastNet.

### Cited Findings
**Evaluations**

(arXiv Sep 2024; AIES 2025) evaluated FourCastNet v1, FourCastNet v2-small, GraphCast-operational and Pangu on Northern Hemisphere TCs, May-Nov 2023, 7-day forecasts, under NHC verification. Findings: track errors and detection rates are "comparable to the best-performing operational models"; adding these models cuts consensus track error by up to 11%. ### Inferences
- **Intensity.** Even 0.25-degree 6-hourly models with millions of training samples weaken TCs. At 1 degree with daily means, ERA5's own Amphan minimum pressure is already much shallower than observed. So realistic depth targets for us are ERA5-daily depths (around 988-1000 hPa daily-mean), not best-track intensity. A post-hoc correction in the AIFS-TC style (a tiny regressor that deepens the MSLP minimum and raises wind near a detected low) is deterministic and CPU-trivial. However, with only about 4,700 days and a handful of Bay of Bengal/Arabian Sea TCs in 2005-2017, any TC-specific head risks overfitting. Up-weight TC days (as AIFS-TC up-weights rapid intensification) rather than building a separate model.
- **Track.** The big models' track skill comes from large-scale steering and vorticity advection being right. The lever for us is transport, not resolution: PARADIS matches 0.25-degree models on track at 1 degree (see Q3).
- WeatherNext Cyclones' trick of co-training on a tabular IBTrACS track is not applicable without adding IBTrACS as a target. It is possible, but adds a data dependency and most likely too few NIO samples.

## Q3. How do models make cyclones translate rather than decay in place (coarse resolution, daily steps)?

### Takeaway
There is direct 2026 evidence that local CNNs cannot move features farther than their receptive field per step. In PARADIS, a 3-layer U-Net (about 44k parameters) failed to move a Gaussian tracer, while a 20-parameter neural semi-Lagrangian layer moved it accurately. A full 1-degree model built on it matched GraphCast, Pangu and HRES on 2020 TC position error. The fix that fits our constraints is a learned semi-Lagrangian advection step inside the network, combined with a local CNN by operator splitting. It replaces the choice between the advection model and the CNN with one model that does both. NowcastNet uses the same "advection operator + learned residual" principle for rain.

### Cited Findings
- **PARADIS, "Learning to Advect: A Neural Semi-Lagrangian Architecture for Weather Forecasting"** (Pereira, Gaudreault, Subich et al., ECCC/UBC; arXiv 2601.21151, Jan 2026). Details are from the arXiv HTML, first 100k characters read.
  - Neural semi-Lagrangian (NSL) operator: a depthwise-separable conv "velocity network" predicts transport fields from the latent state; departure points x_d = x - Δt·u(x); values are sampled by **bicubic** interpolation (4x4 stencil; chosen over bilinear to limit numerical diffusion). Advection runs in a reduced channel subspace. Cost is linear in grid points, vs quadratic for attention.
  - Each step uses **Lie-Trotter splitting** over N_sub sub-steps: velocity, then advection, then diffusion (depthwise 3x3 convs plus a low-rank bias), then reaction (pointwise convs + Swish), each as a residual update. The model predicts 6-h increments from the current and previous states.
  - Idealised test: a 3-layer U-Net (about 44,000 parameters) could not move a Gaussian tracer beyond its receptive field; an NSL layer with 20 learnable parameters relocated it accurately. The authors argue that fixed conv receptive fields force long-range transport through deep stacks, adding diffusive bias.
  - Resolution 1 degree (ERA5 1990-2019; test 2020). Training about 0.8 GPU-months on H100s; 10-day forecast about 3.5 GPU-seconds.
  - Curriculum: reversed-Huber loss, then autoregressive rollouts extended from 12 h to 72 h in 6 stages, then spectral (AMSE) fine-tuning.
  - Results at 1 degree vs 0.25-degree baselines: z500 RMSE at 10 days 727 (PARADIS) vs 729 (GraphCast), 784 (Pangu), 800 (HRES); t850 3.33 vs 3.38; w850 0.181 vs 0.195; q700 worse (1.72e-3 vs 1.64e-3).
  - TCs: across more than 80 storms in 2020, mean position error lower than or comparable to GraphCast, Pangu and HRES (numbers not shown in the part read). Initial intensity error is larger because cores are poorly resolved at 1 degree. Ablation numbers (Appendix G) were not read.
  - — [arXiv 2601.21151](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21151v1)
- Sahu et al. (snippet): ML TC track skill is linked to capturing vorticity advection; GraphCast and Pangu similar, FourCastNet v2 slightly weaker. — [JGR MLC 2025](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2025JH000594) (unverified details)
- NowcastNet's evolution network handles advection explicitly and the generative net handles convection. Motivation: data-driven models violate advective conservation. — [Nature 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06184-4.pdf); [arXiv 2311.17961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.17961v1)
- DGMR (pure conv generative) shows "unnatural motion" and dissipation of extremes at longer leads, which is consistent with weak transport in conv-only designs. — per search snippet referencing [NowcastNet](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10356617)

### Inferences
- **Why our CNN kept Amphan stationary.** Amphan went from about 13.5N to 20.5N over its last days, roughly 2-3 degrees/day, i.e. 2-3 cells per daily step. A CNN whose effective receptive field is a few cells, trained with MSE on a few TC days, minimises loss by keeping a smeared low in place and letting it decay. That is the "blurry mean" solution, and it fits the PARADIS tracer result. It also explains rain persisting a day after the storm left: the rain field is tied to the stationary low/TCWV blob rather than transported.
- **Recommended architecture (fits all constraints).** Per daily step:
  1. Predict a displacement field d = Δt·u_steer per cell from a small conv net on the current state. Initialise or regularise it toward a smoothed steering proxy (e.g. a spatially smoothed 10 m wind or an MSLP-gradient-derived flow, since we lack 500-700 hPa steering winds).
  2. Semi-Lagrangian-advect all 6 fields (or a latent subset) with bicubic interpolation, in 2-4 sub-steps of 6-12 h each.
  3. Apply a residual local CNN for sources and sinks (intensification, rain generation, diurnal-free daily adjustment).
  This is PARADIS's splitting at our scale. It merges our "learned semi-Lagrangian" model (good track, 19.5N/988 hPa) with the CNN (good overall score) instead of choosing between them.
- **Locality tests.** Clip |d| ≤ 8 cells/day (physically, steering flow up to about 10 m/s ≈ 8 degrees/day is ample; 2-4 cells is typical). The departure-point stencil plus a 3x3 residual CNN then keeps the day-1 response local and bounded by construction. That is more guaranteed than a deep U-Net or global attention, both of which risk violating the 8-cells/day rule.
- **CPU cost.** A 32x36 grid with bicubic gather (grid_sample) is microseconds per call. Ten steps x 4 sub-steps is far under 5 s. It is deterministic.
- **Training.** Use PARADIS-style multi-step rollout fine-tuning (e.g. 1 to 3 to 5 days) so the model learns that a stationary storm is penalised over several days. Single-step MSE alone rewards decaying in place.
- **Data.** With only about 4,700 days the velocity net should be tiny (PARADIS shows 20 parameters suffice to learn pure transport). Few-parameter displacement heads are less prone to overfit than enlarging the CNN.

### Gaps
- No quantitative ablation (advection vs no advection) read from PARADIS Appendix G.
- No paper found studying TC translation specifically at daily time steps or with surface-only variables. The steering-proxy choice (10 m wind vs MSLP gradient) for us is an untested inference.
- Not researched: attention or multi-scale (e.g. U-Net with pooled global context) alternatives in a head-to-head on TC translation.

## Q4. Wind-to-rain coupling: enforcing moisture flux convergence or conservation

### Takeaway
Recent work couples rain to dynamics mainly through (a) moisture-flux or moisture-budget terms as soft loss penalties or inputs (PS2F-Net GRL 2026; MZ-Rain 2026), and (b) structural closure where precipitation is diagnosed from a predicted moisture tendency (CondensNet, npj 2025). For our surface-only variables, the closest feasible version is an input feature or soft penalty based on column-moisture flux convergence approximated from TCWV and 10 m winds. This is an approximation and should be treated as one.

### Cited Findings
- PS2F-Net (Wang et al., GRL 2026): extended-range precipitation over East Asia, with vertically integrated moisture flux embedded as a **soft constraint** in the loss. Skill numbers not extracted. — [GRL 2026, 10.1029/2025GL120379](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025GL120379?af=R)
- MZ-Rain (arXiv 2026): a moisture-budget-guided, **zero-inflated** station-level nowcasting model. A "moisture transport and convergence pathway" is driven by diagnosed vertically integrated moisture-flux convergence (VIMFC). This combines a hurdle-type head with budget guidance, close to our design. — [arXiv 2609.04864](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04864)
- Event-aware precipitation forecasting (arXiv 2026) uses the moisture budget as a weak regulariser in training only (not an inference input). — [arXiv 2610.11211](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.11211)
- CondensNet (npj Clim Atmos Sci 2025): precipitation is obtained by vertically integrating the predicted water-vapour tendency, so it is tied to moisture change and closes the water cycle; it gives stable long hybrid climate runs. — [npj 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41612-025-01269-5); [arXiv 2502.13185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13185)
- Hydrology analogue (J. Hydrometeorology 2023): compares soft, hard and hybrid water-balance enforcement; an unconstrained model violated the balance by about 5 mm/day on average. — [JHM 25(1)](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/hydr/25/1/JHM-D-23-0073.1.xml)
- Review (Atmosphere 2025) recommends reporting constrained and unconstrained residuals with units. — [doi 10.3390/atmos17100955](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos17100955)

### Inferences
- **Approximate column budget for our 6 variables:** P - E ≈ -∂(TCWV)/∂t - ∇·(TCWV·V). We lack evaporation and the true vertically integrated flux; 10 m wind is a weak proxy for the moisture-weighted (about 850 hPa) wind. Two uses:
  1. **Input feature:** compute C = -∇·(TCWV · V10) and -ΔTCWV with centred differences (3x3 stencil, local, so it does not break locality) and feed them to the rain head. This links wind changes to rain changes directly, and when the storm's convergence zone moves, the rain feature moves with it. That attacks the "rain persists after the storm left" failure.
  2. **Soft penalty:** add λ·|P_pred - max(0, C - ΔTCWV)|² weighted only on wet cells, or a one-sided penalty for rain where predicted convergence is strongly negative (divergent, drying). Keep λ small: the proxy is biased, and a hard constraint would impose that bias. Learn a per-cell scale and offset (to absorb E and the 10 m vs 850 hPa wind mismatch) rather than using the raw identity.
- Advecting rain with the semi-Lagrangian step (Q3) plus a convergence-gated rain source is the NowcastNet-style decomposition (transport + local generation) at daily scale.
- Hard structural closure in the CondensNet style (rain = -ΔTCWV + convergence) is likely too restrictive with surface-only inputs and daily means; prefer soft.
